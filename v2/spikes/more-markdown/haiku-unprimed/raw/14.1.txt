**Abstract**

Deploying machine learning models on edge devices is constrained by limited memory, which often forces systems to reload model weights and intermediate results from slower storage. Standard cache eviction policies such as least-recently-used (LRU) and least-frequently-used (LFU) were designed for general-purpose workloads and do not account for the predictable, layered access patterns of neural network inference. We propose a lightweight eviction policy that weights cached tensors by their position in the computation graph and their expected reuse distance. We evaluate the policy on three convolutional and transformer-based models running on a constrained embedded platform. Across these workloads, the proposed policy reduces cache miss rates by up to 31% relative to LRU and reduces end-to-end latency by 12–18%, with negligible overhead in bookkeeping. These results suggest that domain-aware eviction can meaningfully improve inference performance without additional hardware.

**1. Introduction**

Edge computing moves inference closer to the data source, reducing network latency and preserving privacy. However, edge devices typically offer a fraction of the memory available on datacenter accelerators, and many must run several models concurrently. When the working set of tensors exceeds available cache, the runtime must repeatedly evict and reload data, and these transfers often dominate inference time.

Cache management in most systems relies on general-purpose heuristics. LRU assumes that recently used data is likely to be used again soon, and LFU assumes that frequently used data remains valuable. Neither assumption holds cleanly for neural network inference. A forward pass touches each layer's weights exactly once in a fixed order, so the most recently used tensor is often the one that will not be needed again until the next inference request. As a result, LRU tends to evict precisely the data that will be needed soonest in the following pass.

This paper makes three contributions. First, we characterize the access patterns of common inference workloads and show that reuse distance is largely determined by layer position. Second, we introduce a retention score that combines static graph structure with observed timing and use it to guide eviction. Third, we implement the policy in a minimal runtime and evaluate it on representative models. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work on caching and memory management for learning systems. Section 3 describes the access model and the proposed policy. Section 4 presents our experimental setup and results, and Section 5 concludes.
